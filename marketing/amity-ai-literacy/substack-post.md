## Post body (paste from here down)

In August 2025, before we ran a single training session in Amity, Oregon, we sent the district's teachers a survey. One question asked what worried them most about AI in their classrooms.

The answer, over and over, was cheating.

Not all of it was about homework. A high school math teacher wrote something we have thought about ever since: *"What does pervasive AI use say about the culture we have created in our schools?"* A middle school ELA teacher pointed out that many students already struggle with literacy, and that research links heavy AI use to weaker critical thinking.

Those are serious objections. They deserved a real answer, not a pep talk about productivity.

Seven months later, an eighth grader in the same district wrote this on an exit ticket:

> "ChatGPT is a tool. I'm the one who still has to think."

This post is about what happened in between. Superintendent Jeff Clark has reviewed the full case study and cleared us to share it, so we can finally tell the story with the real numbers.

## A small district made an unusual call

Amity is a rural district of about 756 students in Oregon's Willamette Valley. Teachers there wear several hats. There is no AI department and no plan to hire one.

When AI showed up in its classrooms, the district had the same two options everyone has. Write a policy and hope enforcement holds, or buy a student program and hand it to whoever has room in their schedule.

Superintendent Clark picked neither. Before any student curriculum, the district trained its teachers. Forty educators went through our Tomorrow's AI Champions program in August 2025, in two cohorts. The room included a first-year science teacher, a social studies teacher with 31 years in the classroom, a wood shop instructor, special education and agriculture teachers, and a principal. In the pre-training survey, Clark wrote: *"My teachers and our students will benefit from this PD."*

The reasoning is simple once you say it out loud. Students take their cues about technology from adults they trust. If those adults are anxious about AI, or quietly using it and never talking about it, no curriculum will fix that. A visiting specialist leaves on Friday. The classroom teacher is back on Monday.

After the training, 93% of the educators we surveyed said they understood AI in education better than before. The signal we liked more came from Mary Matocha, principal of Amity Middle School: *"Post Tomo AI Training, educators are sharing new AI tools or strategies with each other."* When teachers start trading tips with each other, the training has stuck.

## Where the students started

The student program began in October 2025. Two Amity teachers led it: Darcy Hatch with eighth graders starting in October, and Jill Whitehead with ninth graders starting in January. That came to 132 students, 15 sessions per grade, 60 minutes each.

Before the ninth-grade sessions began, 65 freshmen took a baseline survey. Two numbers from it explain why the program needed to exist:

- **82%** could tell helpful AI from harmful AI.
- **25%** felt capable of creating anything with it.

These kids grew up with recommendation feeds and chatbots. They knew what AI was. What they lacked was any sense that they had a say in it. Only 40% felt confident explaining AI to a friend, and 43% said they weren't excited about it at all. That last number surprised us. It read less like fear and more like a shrug, as if AI were something that happened to them.

We started calling this the agency gap: high awareness, low agency. More screen time doesn't close it. Understanding how the thing works might.

## What a session actually looked like

Every lesson ran 60 minutes, was built to be taught by a regular classroom teacher rather than a computer science specialist, and had students doing something with their hands.

Eighth graders trained their own image classifiers in Teachable Machine. They played Semantris, a word game built on a language model, to see how AI connects meaning. They practiced a prompting framework and then tested what happens to an answer when you change the context around the question. Ninth graders audited their own TikTok feeds to work out what the algorithm had decided about them.

Running through all of it was an ethics thread we call Navigating Self. Ethics wasn't a unit tacked onto the end of the semester. Each week carried its own question: who owns what you make with AI, what happens to your data, who benefits and who pays, when trusting a system is reasonable and when it's just lazy.

One student summed up the image classifier lesson better than we could:

> "It's weird that my face can teach a computer to see. We trained our own image classifier and I actually get it now."

## The numbers, including the dip

Every eighth-grade session ended with an exit ticket scored from 1 to 5. As of March 2026, with 10 sessions of data in:

- **4.25 out of 5** average score across those 10 sessions
- **87%** average pass rate, counting a 3 or higher as a pass
- **4.6 out of 5** for the highest-scoring session, the Ethics Debate, where 94% passed

The low point was session 10, Context & Constraints, at a 78% pass rate. That's where prompting gets hard. You're no longer just asking a question; you're learning how framing, constraints and missing context change what comes back. It was the most demanding material in the sequence and the scores showed it. The next two sessions came in at 92% and 94%.

We're showing you the dip on purpose. A report where every number climbs should make you suspicious. This class had a hard week and recovered from it.

The ninth graders were measured differently, through written reflections and session ratings. Their favorite session was The Algorithm of You, the TikTok feed audit, rated 4.8 out of 5. Teenagers, it turns out, are very interested in finding out what an algorithm thinks of them.

## What the kids wrote

The numbers get a superintendent's attention. The written responses are what convinced us. These are from exit tickets, lightly edited for clarity.

On how AI works:

> "I used to think AI just knew everything. Now I know it was trained — someone chose what it learned." *(8th grade)*

On prompting:

> "I thought prompting was just typing questions. Now I know the way you frame something totally changes the answer." *(8th grade)*

On privacy:

> "If I don't read the privacy policy, I'm basically giving someone else permission to use my data however they want." *(8th grade)*

On the debate:

> "The ethics debate was hard because both sides had a real point. That's what real decisions feel like." *(8th grade)*

And on the thing their teachers were worried about back in August:

> "Use AI for ideas not for answers." *(9th grade)*

> "AI is a smart tool that makes things easier for you but makes your brain slower if you rely on it too much." *(9th grade)*

Put those last two next to the teacher survey. The teachers were afraid AI would do their students' thinking for them. Within their first few sessions, ninth graders had arrived at the same worry and written it down in their own words. Nobody made them sign an honor pledge. They got there by learning how the tool works and noticing what it costs them to lean on it.

That's the closest thing we have to an answer to the cheating question. You can't block your way there. A student who knows the chatbot was trained on someone else's choices, and who has felt their own thinking get lazier when they lean on it, has a reason to hold back that no content filter can give them.

## What we'd tell another district

If you're a superintendent or curriculum lead weighing AI literacy for next year, here's what Amity's year suggests.

**Train the adults first.** Amity trained 40 educators in August and launched the student program in October. By then, the people teaching it had already worked through their own doubts.

**Don't make it a computer science course.** The lessons were designed so any teacher could lead them. In a district Amity's size, that's the difference between a program that happens and one that doesn't.

**Put ethics in every lesson.** A standalone ethics unit gets forgotten by spring. A question that comes back every week turns into a habit. Amity's highest-scoring eighth-grade session was a debate with no right answer.

**Measure every session, and show the bad weeks too.** Weekly exit tickets tell you where students are struggling while the course is still running, not in a report after it ends. Publishing the dips is what makes the rest of the numbers believable.

## What comes next

The numbers here run through March 2026, when the case study was written. The course was designed to end with a capstone project, where each student picks a real problem and prototypes an AI solution for it, and with post-surveys measured against the same baseline.
